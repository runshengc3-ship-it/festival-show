Telling Chinese Stories to the World: Cultural Soft Power and Confidence on Screen

A festival exhibition exploring how five Chinese films, across five distinct genres, speak to the world through attraction, resonance, memory, and emotional imagination.

Kung Fu Action Science Fiction Disaster Modern Warfare Historical Drama Fantasy Romance

Festival Theme

A confident culture never speaks in only one voice. This exhibition presents five cinematic beams of light that together project a plural, multidimensional, and self-assured image of China onto the global screen.

Curatorial Statement

Theme Statement

A confident culture never speaks in only one voice.

When Chinese cinema goes out into the world, what it carries is not a single face, but a kaleidoscope of cinematic storytelling. Under the theme Telling Chinese Stories to the World: Cultural Soft Power and Confidence on Screen, this film festival carefully selects five outstanding works that span kung fu action, science-fiction disaster, modern warfare, historical drama, and fantasy romance. Like five beams of different colors, they jointly tell the world of a China that is diverse, multidimensional, and confident.

This cultural confidence takes on entirely different faces:

  • It is the Wing Chun wooden-dummy stance of a grandmaster, using the Eastern wisdom of “ending violence through martial virtue” to converse with the world and reveal the soft power of traditional culture.
  • It is the ten thousand engines pushing the Earth across the frozen plains, responding to humanity’s shared destiny with the tenderness of “taking our home with us,” and demonstrating the universal force of Eastern values.
  • It is the figure of a veteran soldier standing fast at the national border, interpreting the responsibility of a great nation through the act of protecting compatriots, and showing the persuasive power of national spirit.
  • It is the smiles and tears of seven ordinary people, redefining grand narrative through human warmth and telling Chinese stories in a way that is soft yet resilient.
  • It is an Eastern love story that crosses the boundary between human and demon, questioning universal emotion through reflections on outward appearance and true feeling, and completing a cross-cultural dialogue in the most poetic way.

Five genres, five voices, five on-screen echoes of cultural soft power. No longer do these films wait passively to be gazed upon. Instead, they actively enter the mainstream arena of global cinema: using kung fu to converse with tradition, science fiction to embrace the future, war to write responsibility, history to warm the human heart, and love to cross borders. This is precisely the core of what Joseph Nye calls soft power: not imposing influence through coercion, but winning sincere resonance through attraction.

Telling Chinese stories to the world means showing the world cultural confidence. This edition of the festival invites you into the cinema to listen to these five stories and witness a China that is speaking with composure and resounding with confidence.

Selected Films

Five Films and the Curatorial Rationale

1. Ip Man (2008)

Kung Fu Action: The Soft Power of Martial Spirit and Cultural Dignity

Director: Wilson Yip | Genre: Kung Fu / Biographical

In Foshan in the 1930s, the Japanese army invades, and the homeland falls into ruin. Ip Man, a Wing Chun grandmaster, falls from privilege into poverty, yet in a moment of national peril he steps forward and uses his fists to protect the dignity of his people. From the calm discipline of training before the wooden dummy to his resolute confrontation with a Japanese officer in the arena, Ip Man tells the world through Wing Chun that true strength does not lie in knocking down an opponent, but in ending violence through martial virtue and winning respect through moral character.

Within the framework of a commercial kung fu film, this work injects a distinctly Eastern heroism: restrained, righteous, and composed. It turns kung fu from an exotic Eastern spectacle into a narrative subject that carries cultural soft power. When Ip Man calmly rolls up his sleeves and accepts the fight, what he shows the world is the martial philosophy embedded in Chinese traditional culture: that “to stop the spear is true martiality.” This spiritual force of winning hearts through virtue is far more enduring and far more persuasive than the destructive force of fists and feet.

International Presentation: The Ip Man series has been distributed to more than eighty countries and regions worldwide, becoming the most internationally recognizable Chinese kung fu IP after Bruce Lee and Jackie Chan. Through Eastern martial philosophy, it completes a cultural dialogue with global action-film audiences and stands as an exemplary work of traditional cultural soft power telling Chinese stories to the world.

2. The Wandering Earth (2019)

Science Fiction Disaster: The Soft Power of Homeland Consciousness and Humanity’s Shared Destiny

Director: Frant Gwo | Genre: Science Fiction / Disaster

As the sun begins to expand and threatens to engulf the Earth, humanity launches the “Wandering Earth” project, building ten thousand engines across the planet’s surface to push the entire Earth out of the solar system. While Western science fiction often imagines elites boarding spacecraft to seek a new home, this landmark Chinese science-fiction film offers an entirely different solution: take the Earth with us.

This premise is rooted in Chinese civilization’s deep attachment to the land, yet through the globally intelligible language of hard science fiction it touches a shared emotional question for all humankind: What is home, and what makes a home? The film shows the world a distinctly Chinese answer: in the face of crisis, do not abandon and do not give up; live and perish together with one’s homeland. This is the deeper core of China’s cultural soft power: not to conquer through economic or military force, but to inspire and resonate through a value system built upon a “community with a shared future.”

International Presentation: When The Wandering Earth was released in North America, Australia, and New Zealand, it sparked wide discussion. Netflix acquired its global streaming rights, and international media hailed it as the beginning of “the first year of Chinese science-fiction cinema.” It proves that Chinese stories can use the most international genre language to communicate the most distinctive cultural values, making it a successful practice of telling Chinese stories to the world.

3. Wolf Warrior (2015)

Modern Warfare: The Soft Power of Great-Power Responsibility and the Spirit of Protection

Director: Wu Jing | Genre: Action / War

Leng Feng, a retired special forces soldier, encounters an incursion by foreign mercenaries during a border exercise. When his comrades and his country’s territory are threatened, he does not retreat. Instead, he turns his iron will into a blade and blocks the enemy at the national frontier. “Whoever offends China will be punished, no matter how far away they are.” This line, which runs through the whole film, declares within the framework of a standard action blockbuster a narrative posture that no longer avoids the confidence of a great nation.

The Chinese story this film tells the world is not a narrative of conquest through intimidation, but a value narrative of protection and responsibility. Leng Feng fights not to invade, but to protect; not to expand, but to defend. This form of cultural soft power, centered on protection, shapes the image of a responsible and trustworthy great power. China’s strength here is presented as the source of every ordinary citizen’s sense of security, rather than as a threat to others.

International Presentation: Wolf Warrior became the first Chinese-language film from China to enter the global top 100 box office chart, while its sequel Wolf Warrior 2 set an Asian box office record with 870 million US dollars. Through the international grammar of the action genre, it completed the global circulation of a Chinese-style heroic narrative and demonstrated both the cultural confidence and market appeal of Chinese cinema.

4. My People, My Country (2019)

Historical Drama: The Soft Power of Individual Warmth within Collective Memory

Directors: Chen Kaige, Zhang Yibai, Guan Hu, Xue Xiaolu, Xu Zheng, Ning Hao, Wen Muye | Genre: Drama / Anthology

Seven historical moments, seven stories of ordinary people. On the eve of the founding ceremony of the People’s Republic, an engineer stays awake all night for an electric flag-raising device. When the women’s volleyball team wins the championship, a Shanghai alley erupts in celebration at a single cheer. At the opening of the Beijing Olympics, one ticket links together the joys and sorrows of everyday people. Created collectively by seven directors, this anthology film transforms the grand narrative of seventy years of New China into the smiles and tears found in streets, alleys, and daily life, with the touch of a prose poem.

The cultural confidence this film shows the world is a soft yet profound force. It no longer tries to argue on the scale of grand narrative that “we are great,” but calmly presents “who we are”: ordinary people living seriously and warming one another amid the currents of history. This narrative attitude, in which the extraordinary is witnessed through the ordinary, is precisely the most moving form of cultural soft power. It is not a top-down declaration, but a level-eyed sharing with the world.

International Presentation: When the film was released in North America, Europe, Australia, and other regions, its distinctive structure and universal emotional expression aroused curiosity and understanding among overseas Chinese audiences and foreign viewers alike toward contemporary Chinese history. It tells the world a Chinese story full of warmth: the greatness of a nation is visible in the smiles and tears of ordinary people.

5. Painted Skin (2008)

Fantasy Romance: The Soft Power of Eastern Emotional Philosophy and Human Dilemmas

Director: Gordon Chan | Genre: Fantasy / Romance

Against the vast desert and solitary smoke, the fox spirit Xiaowei falls in love with the general Wang Sheng. Yet Wang Sheng already has a wife, Peirong. In order to obtain this love, Xiaowei uses demonic arts to bewitch human hearts, while Peirong willingly transforms herself into a white-haired monster to prove her innocence. When outward appearance and true feeling no longer align, and when love and possession become difficult to distinguish, an Eastern inquiry into the meaning of love slowly unfolds within a spectacle of fantasy.

Adapted from Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio, the film wraps profound reflection on “emotion” and “righteousness,” “desire” and “virtue,” in the shell of a supernatural romance. When Xiaowei finally withdraws her outstretched hand and dissolves into mist, the film reveals an Eastern philosophy of love: that love means fulfillment rather than possession. In this way, it shows the world a distinctive Chinese emotional philosophy. The film proves that China’s cultural soft power need not always rely on grand historical narratives. A strange and passionate love that crosses the boundary between human and demon can likewise complete a cross-cultural emotional dialogue in the most poetic way.

International Presentation: Painted Skin created a phenomenon-level viewing craze in East and Southeast Asia, and successfully entered mainstream markets in Europe and North America. Through its combination of Eastern aesthetics and universally recognizable emotional dilemmas, it showed the world a Chinese story that is both rooted in tradition and capable of touching humanity’s shared feelings.

Narrative Arc

The Shared Spectrum of the Five Films

From the martial arts school in Foshan to the frozen Earth, from China’s borderlands to Beijing’s hutongs, and then to a strange romance deep in the desert that crosses the boundary between human and demon, these five films of strikingly different genres together form a complete spectrum: they stand through traditional martial virtue, reflect through visions of the future, write through great-power responsibility, carry through individual warmth, and suffuse through Eastern emotional philosophy.

Together they answer one question posed by our time: What is cultural soft power? It is the wisdom of martial arts in kung fu, the deep feeling for homeland in science fiction, the responsibility of protection in war, the warmth of individuals in history, and the philosophy of fulfillment in love. It is to attract, to inspire, and to resonate.

This is the confident echo of culture on screen: Chinese stories told to the world with calmness, richness, and conviction.
